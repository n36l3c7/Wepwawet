Methodology

How Wepwawet turns public data and network measurements into an estimate, and where each technique stops being reliable.

1. Passive evidence

Before measuring anything, Wepwawet asks the sources that already know something about the address: GeoIP databases (DB-IP Lite, IPinfo Lite, MaxMind GeoLite2, and GeoLite2 through RIPEstat), RIPE IPmap, the routing table (RIPEstat: prefix, origin AS, RIR country), the registration (RDAP), reverse DNS of the address and of its /24 neighbours, PeeringDB (where the origin network is present), Shodan InternetDB, and optionally Shodan and Censys.

GeoIP databases are often right for hosting and often wrong for small or re-assigned blocks, and they disagree with each other more often than one expects: the disagreement is reported in kilometres. Registration country is where the holder is registered, not where the address is used. These answers are soft evidence: hints, never limits.

2. Distance from delay

A packet cannot travel faster than light in fibre, about 200 km per millisecond one way (two thirds of c). A minimum round-trip time of rtt ms from a probe therefore bounds the target to 

d_max = rtt / 2 × 200 km

The minimum over several packets is used because queueing only ever adds delay. Real paths are longer than the great circle and cross routers, so the true distance is usually well inside the bound: the bound is safe but loose. When the target drops ICMP, the TCP handshake time to port 443 is measured instead.

Calibration (optional). Constraint-Based Geolocation (Gueye et al., 2006) tightens the bound per probe: each probe pings landmarks of known position (RIPE Atlas anchors) and Wepwawet fits the "bestline", the line rtt = m·d + b that stays below every landmark with the slope at least the physical minimum. Inverted, it bounds the distance more tightly than the speed of light alone.

3. The ping campaign

A global sweep pings the target from about 48 Globalping probes on every continent; the fastest probes are near it. A regional zoom then uses about 30 probes in the fastest probe's country and in the countries the passive sources name, which gives bounds of tens of kilometres when a probe is in the same city. A local zoom follows with about 20 probes in the region's most likely city and in the target's own autonomous system: a probe of the same operator reaches the target through the operator's own local network, so its RTT is the shortest available, which matters most for residential lines. Every phase respects a test budget and Globalping's hourly limit.

Most home routers answer neither ping nor TCP. Then a few traceroutes from the country the passive sources name find the operator's last public router on the way to the address (the same autonomous system, the router most paths share, the nearest to the address among equals), and the whole campaign measures that router instead. Paths from different places enter the operator at different points and meet only near the address, while the last router a single path shows can be a backbone router elsewhere when the hops after it stay silent; with several paths and no router shared by two of them, nothing stands in. The router is where the line leaves the operator's network, so the uncertainty grows by 30 km for the line beyond it, and the report says which router stood in.

4. The feasible region

Every probe draws a disk of radius d_max; the target is in all of them. The intersection is computed on Uber's H3 hexagonal grid, coarse to fine: start from every resolution-1 cell, keep the cells whose centre is within each disk plus the cell size, replace them with their children, and stop when the region would need more than 15 000 cells or reaches resolution 8 (cells of about 0.7 km²). The region's area is the sum of its cells' areas; its centre is the spherical mean of the cells; the uncertainty radius is the 90th-percentile distance from the centre plus a cell. GeoNames cities above 15 000 inhabitants inside the region are listed and ranked by size and closeness to the centre.

5. Anycast

If two probes far apart (more than 500 km) are both so fast that their disks cannot overlap, no single place satisfies both: the address answers from several places. Wepwawet reports the speed-of-light violations, lists the sites (the tightest mutually disjoint disks) and computes the region of the site nearest the fastest probe only.

A probe whose declared position is wrong produces the same symptom: it conflicts with nearly every other probe. So before calling an address anycast, Wepwawet removes the probes with the most conflicts, at most 10 % of them; if every conflict then disappears, those probes are reported as misplaced and ignored. A probe answering in under 10 ms is never removed this way: so fast an answer is what a real anycast site produces. In the benchmark, a probe declared in Pretoria answering an anchor in Winterthur in 42 ms was caught like this.

6. Traceroutes and router names

The five fastest probes trace the path to the target. Router hostnames often name a place: an operator's own code (ffm-bb2.twelve99.net is Frankfurt), an airport or IATA metro code (fra1, mil04s50), a UN/LOCODE (itmil01) or a city (amsterdam). Operator conventions live in geohint_rules.toml, in the spirit of Hoiho (Luckie et al., 2021). Three-letter tokens are ambiguous, so router words are ignored and every hint is checked against the hop's own RTT: a router 2 ms from the probe cannot be 1 000 km away, and such a hint is discarded. Routers inside an exchange point's peering LAN (PeeringDB) are placed at that exchange.

The router nearest the target that most paths share is the convergence point: with its location and the RTT left from it to the target, it is a strong anchor. Private addresses are never treated as shared, since two 10.0.0.1 are different routers.

7. The access line

The RTT step from the last public router to the target is the access line: under 0.5 ms looks like a data centre, 1 to 3 ms like fibre, 3 to 15 ms like cable or xDSL, more like mobile or fixed wireless (Wang et al., 2011, use the same idea for landmark-based refinement). Every probe's RTT includes this delay, so 80 % of it is removed from every RTT before the region is recomputed; the 20 % margin keeps the region from shrinking past the target when one path had a slower last mile. The correction never exceeds 80 % of the fastest RTT, which contains the whole access delay, and it is not applied to a router standing in for a silent address: there the step is the time the router takes to answer a ping, which a disk must not lose.

Landmarks (experimental)

With measurements.landmarks set, RIPE Atlas probes inside the region, which publish their position and public address, become landmarks: half of them on the target's own network when there are any, the rest spread over the region. The nearest probes that measured the target (the local zoom) ping each landmark, and every landmark gets a score, the median over the shared probes of |RTT to the target − RTT to the landmark|. A landmark next to the target, on the same paths, scores near zero: this is the delay-space idea of GeoPing (Padmanabhan and Subramanian, 2001), applied only inside the region the disks already allow, in the spirit of the landmark refinement of Wang et al. (2011). The three best landmarks, weighted by 1 / (score + 0.2 ms), give a soft answer whose accuracy is their spread (at least 5 km). It is experimental: landmark positions are set by their hosts, many home probes do not answer pings, and two places in one city can sit behind very different paths, so the answer only ranks places inside the region and never moves the hard limit.

8. Fusion and confidence

The RTT region is a hard limit. Inside it, every candidate place (the cities in the region and the soft answers that fall in it) is scored by the soft evidence that agrees with it: 

score(c) = Σ weight(e) · exp(−d(c, e)² / 2σ(e)²)     σ(e) = max(25 km, accuracy of e)

The best candidate is the estimate, except when the region's radius is 50 km or less: then the measurements are sharper than any GeoIP answer (which is a city centre), so the region's centre is kept and the best candidate only names the place. Soft answers outside the region are contradicted by physics, dropped and listed as ruled out. Confidence starts from the area of the region (100 km² gives about 85, 10 000 km² about 55), gains 6 points per independent agreeing source (up to 4), is capped at 30 for anycast, and loses points for mobile (15), VPN (10), CDN (10) and hosting (5) networks. Without measurements it starts at 10 and never exceeds 38.

9. Accuracy

wepwawet benchmark locates RIPE Atlas anchors, whose positions RIPE NCC publishes, and reports for each stage (passive, after the pings, after the traceroutes, final) the median and 90th-percentile error and how often the truth fell inside the stated uncertainty. Anchors live in data centres and well-connected networks, where GeoIP is good and probes are near; residential and mobile addresses are harder, so treat the benchmark as a best case. RIPE IPmap is built partly on RIPE Atlas data and knows the anchors well, so passive results on anchors are flattering; run the benchmark with [providers.ipmap] enabled = false for a fairer passive figure.

Passive sources only, 20 anchors (seed 1), November 2026:

Sources Median error 90th percentile Truth inside the uncertainty
All passive sources 3.6 km 13 km 95 %
Without RIPE IPmap 5.8 km 180 km 75 %

With measurements, two anchors at 84 Globalping tests each: Singapore was located 0.6 km from its published position (the fastest probe answered in 0.5 ms), Winterthur 14 km; the truth was inside the stated uncertainty at every stage. Two anchors are an example, not a statistic.

10. Limits

  • Residential: the realistic ceiling is the provider's point of presence or BNG. The subscriber's address is only in the provider's records. Latency measures the path to that equipment: one millisecond of round trip is about 100 km of fibre, and a home line adds from under one millisecond (fibre) to tens (xDSL, radio), so no measurement separates two neighbourhoods. Around a large city, a radius of some tens of kilometres is the expected result.
  • Mobile and CGNAT: traffic leaves through a gateway that can be hundreds of kilometres from the user.
  • VPN, Tor, proxies, hosting: the result is the exit server.
  • Anycast: there is no single location.
  • MPLS hides routers; asymmetric routing makes the return path differ from the forward one; ICMP filtering leaves holes in traceroutes. All three widen the uncertainty, and Wepwawet flags them.
  • Probe positions are self-declared or geolocated by the platforms and can be wrong by a few kilometres.
